Automation

When to Automate a Business Process

Automation is not a blanket solution for slow operations. The right candidate is a process that is repetitive, rule-based, and already working — not one that is broken.

The automation trap

The most common mistake in automation is trying to use it to fix a broken process. If your team is struggling because steps are missing, rules change every week, and data is missing, automation will just execute those errors faster. Automating a broken process creates a faster broken process. Always fix the process manually first.

What makes a good automation candidate

The best candidates for automation share three traits. First, they are rule-based. If a human has to use judgment ("this client is usually late, let's wait a day"), it's hard to automate. Second, they have high volume. Spending 40 hours building an automation for a task that takes 5 minutes a month doesn't make sense. Third, the inputs and outputs are consistent.

Common processes worth automating

Many businesses find immediate value in automating data syncs between systems (e.g., when a deal closes in CRM, create a project in the delivery tool), invoice generation and sending, notification triggers for out-of-bounds conditions, and order status updates to clients.

What to map before building

Before writing any code or setting up a Zap, map the current process step by step. Document every click, every decision, and critically, every exception. What happens if the email bounces? What happens if the attachment is missing? Who gets notified when the automation inevitably hits an error state?

The honest ROI conversation

Automation saves time, but it is not free. It has a build cost and an ongoing maintenance cost. APIs change, passwords expire, and business rules evolve. The ROI of automation is found when the hours saved per year significantly outweigh the combined cost of the initial build and the ongoing maintenance required to keep it running smoothly.
